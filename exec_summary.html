Compliance

Compliance Rate [Waist] 56.4% (n=2137) 93.4% (n=318) 21.5% (n=1986)
Compliance Rate [Hip] 54.6% (n=2137) 93.4% (n=318) 8.1% (n=1986)*

 

Table 1 reports the compliance rate across the three modes of data collection. These initial results suggest that self-measurement with an interviewer present elicits a considerably higher compliance rate (93.4%) than the other two modes of collection: 37pp higher than self-measurement and 72pp more than BVI app.

The BVI app shows the lowest respondent uptake, at only 21.5% for waist circumference and 8.1% for hip circumference. Out of the 1986 respondents who were asked to download and use the BVI app, 679 (ie. 34%) reported to have installed and logged in successfully.

However, only 62% of respondents who reported to have installed and logged in the app sent back usable waist data (N=427), and only 24% send back hip measurements (N=160) and only 160 respondents (~ 24% of those who installed the app). It’s not clear what is the cause of the missing data on waist (Nmissing=252) and hip (Nmissing=524) measurements, but it certainly conditions our ability to interpret the compliance rate of installing and using the app, as almost 1/3 of the respondents who report having installing and logging in successfully don’t send back any data.

(*) The figure for hip circumference is not strictly a compliance rate given that the hip circumference measure we have access to is not calculated within the app, but in Select Research servers and presents significant missing data.

The BVI app data provides us two sets of measures: (1) measures calculated within the app using the photo uploaded by the respondent; (2) measures calculated in Select Research servers using a 3D avatar generated from the photo uploaded by the respondent. The distinction between the two sets of measures is important given that we find no missing data in app-based measures and substantial missing data (~70%, see larger report for details) in server-side measures.

For this analysis, in Table 2 we report app-based estimates for waist circumference and server-side estimates for hip circumference (in its current form the app does not display hip circumference to the respondents, but Select Research can extract this data for us in future waves). We should exercise some caution in interpreting the results, given that we’re missing 2/3 of hip circumferences.

Overall, the BVI app data seems to provide less precise estimates for both waist and hip circumferences when compared to self-measurement, with or without the interviewer present (the standard deviation for waist is twice as large in the BVI app compared to the other two).

The most noticeable difference in is waist circumference: the average waist in the BVI app sample is ~23cm wider than the average in CAPI respondents, and almost 30cm wider than the self-measurement group. These differences can’t be accounted for by differences in weight across these samples as the average weight is 79.3kg in the self-measurement group, 79.4kg in the BVI app group and 79kg in the interviewer-led group, with virtually identical standard deviations.

Hip circumference shows less differences across the three modes of data collection. The BVI app sample still shows the highest averages, but much closer to the other two: the difference in average hip circumference is ~2 cm between BVI app and interviewer-led samples, and ~6cm between BVI app and self-measurement samples. The standard deviations do not show significant differences either.

This suggests that the real concern is in getting respondents to measure waist in the right spot.

One caveat in the BVI data is the larger number of outliers when compared to the other two groups, which can account for some differences in the reported means. The average waist circumference for the BVI sample reported in Table 2 was computed after removing removing respondents for whom waist circumference reported was ≥2000cm. Arguably we should set the outlier threshold lower, but even removing reported waist circumferences ≥ 180cm we only lower the average by about 2 cm. The long form of this report provides more granular results on this point.

The self-measurement monitored by the interviewer works as a barometer for the quality of the data gathered, as it offers greater reliability and precision than any of the other two measurements. The last batch of data has raised our N of CAPI respondents from N=21 to N=374, although the subset of CAPI respondents for whom we obtained measurements via the BVI app is still small (N=34 for hip and N=59 for waist circumferences).

Differences in Waist measurements

Consistent with earlier results, the BVI app seems to yield the worst quality data: the average difference in measurements for respondents who used both the tape and the BVI app to measure their waist circumference is around 27cm. The average difference for those respondents who measured with the app and the interviewer present is 18cm. And although the N is smaller for the latter comparison (59 respondents), that is certainly not the case for the former (N=291).

Similar differences in Hip measurements…

  • between interviewer-led and self-measurement, the average difference in hip circumference is 3.4cm and in waist-to-hip ratio is 0.04.

  • between the BVI app and tape self-measurements, the average difference in hip circumference is 11.2 cm (without interviewer present) and 9.1 cm (with interviewer present).

And in Waist-to-Hip ratios

With respect to waist-to-hip ratios, the BVI app bias is 2 times higher than the self-measurement without interviewer present, when we compare both to interviewer-led measurements of waist-to-hip ratio (0.09 vs 0.04).

 

Take home points

 

  1. When comparing self-measurement and BVI app data against interviewer-led measurements, we find that the bias is much smaller for self-measurement vis a vis the BVI app;

  2. Overall, the BVI app data seems to be less accurate its measurement;

  3. The bias in waist measurements tends to be considerably higher than in hip measurements (3 times higher when comparing BVI app with self-measurement).

  4. The lower quality of the BVI app data may be driven by several factors, including the setup of the room where the photos were taken. Only 30% of the respondents who gave us valid BVI data took a picture with an empty wall in the background, and out of those only 60% (74 respondents) did it in front of a white wall. In short, only 17.3% of the sub-sample with valid BVI app estimates took the pictures in the best possible conditions.
