Compliance

Table 1 reports the compliance rate across the three modes of data collection. These initial results suggest that self-measurement with an interviewer present elicits a considerably higher compliance rate (93.4%) than the other two modes of collection: 37pp higher than waist self-measurement in CAWI+CATI respondents, and 73pp more than respondents who sent data back on the BVI app for waist measurement.

The BVI app shows the lowest respondent uptake, at only 22.2% for waist circumference and 7.9% for hip circumference. Out of the 1565 respondents who were asked to download and use the BVI app, 497 (ie. 32%) reported to have installed and logged in successfully. However, only 347 respondents (nearly 70%) send back usable waist data, and only 123 (around 25%) respondents showed valid hip measurements.

At this stage it’s not clear what is the cause of the missing data on waist (Nmissing=150) and hip (Nmissing=224) measurements, but it certainly conditions our ability to interpret the compliance rate of installing and using the app, as almost 1/3 of the respondents who report having installing and logging in successfully don’t send back valid waist and hip data.

The BVI app data seems to provide less precise estimates for waist circumference when compared to self-measurements with or without the interviewer present (the standard deviation for waist is twice as large in the BVI app compared to the other two). The BVI app measures shown in Table 2 are the output that the respondent sees and feeds back to the survey when using the app. The other measurements available shows a large number of missing values: this is probably the case because these measures are server-side computations of waist and hip circumferences by a computer-vision algorithm that uses the photos uploaded by the respondent via the app. One possible explanation for the large number of missing data (around 67% for waist circumference) is that the images uploaded don’t reach the standard of quality that allows the algorithm to accurately compute waist and hip circumferences.

The most noticeable difference in is waist circumference: the average waist in the BVI app sample is ~23cm wider than average of the self-measurement with interviewer group, and almost 30cm wider than the self-measurement group. These differences can’t be explained by sample differences in weight, for example, as the average is average weight is 79.3kg in the self-measurement group, 79.4kg in the BVI app group and 79kg in the interviewer-led measurement group, with virtually identical standard deviations.

Hip circumference shows less differences across the three modes of data collection. The BVI app sample still shows the highest averages, but much closer to the other two: the difference in average hip circumference is ~2 cm between BVI app and interviewer-led measures, and ~6cm between BVI app and self-measurement. The standard deviations do not show significant differences either.

This suggests that the real concern is in getting respondents to measure waist in the right spot.

One caveat in the BVI data is a larger number of outliers when compared to the other analogue modes of self-measurement, which can explain differences in the reported means. However, the average waist circumference for the BVI sample reported in Table 2 was computed after removing one data point for which the waist circumference was 2000cm. The long form of this report provides more granular results on this point.

The self-measurement monitored by the interviewer works as a barometer for the quality of the data gathered, as it offers greater reliability and precision than any of the other two measurements. The last batch of data has raised our N of CAPI respondents from N=21 to N=374, although the subset of CAPI respondents for whom we obtained measurements via the BVI app is still small (N=34 for hip and N=59 for waist circumferences).

Differences in Waist measurements

Consistent with earlier results, the BVI app seems to yield the worst quality data: the average difference in measurements for respondents who used both the tape and the BVI app to measure their waist circumference is around 27cm. The average difference for those respondents who measured with the app and the interviewer present is 18cm. And although the N is smaller for the latter comparison (59 respondents), that is certainly not the case for the former (N=291).

Similar differences in Hip measurements…

  • between interviewer-led and self-measurement, the average difference in hip circumference is 3.4cm and in waist-to-hip ratio is 0.04.

  • between the BVI app and tape self-measurements, the average difference in hip circumference is 11.2 cm (without interviewer present) and 9.1 cm (with interviewer present).

And in Waist-to-Hip ratios

With respect to waist-to-hip ratios, the BVI app bias is 2 times higher than the self-measurement without interviewer present, when we compare both to interviewer-led measurements of waist-to-hip ratio (0.09 vs 0.04).

 

Take home points

 

  1. When comparing self-measurement and BVI app data against interviewer-led measurements, we find that the bias is much smaller for self-measurement vis a vis the BVI app;

  2. Overall, the BVI app data seems to be less accurate its measurement;

  3. The bias in waist measurements tends to be considerably higher than in hip measurements (3 times higher when comparing BVI app with self-measurement).

  4. The lower quality of the BVI app data may be driven by several factors, including the setup of the room where the photos were taken. Only 30% of the respondents who gave us valid BVI data took a picture with an empty wall in the background, and out of those only 60% (74 respondents) did it in front of a white wall. In short, only 17.3% of the sub-sample with valid BVI app estimates took the pictures in the best possible conditions.
